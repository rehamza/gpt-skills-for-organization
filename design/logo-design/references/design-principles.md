# Logo Design Principles

Read this reference before creating, refining, selecting, or critiquing a logo.

## Strategic Fit

A logo must identify the right brand before it expresses a personal aesthetic. Translate the brief into one dominant recognition idea. Align the level of formality, energy, warmth, precision, heritage, or innovation with the audience and market position.

Avoid attempting to communicate every product feature. A logo is a durable identifier; messaging belongs to copy, campaigns, and the wider identity system.

## Distinctiveness

Build a silhouette, rhythm, letter relationship, or structural gesture that can be remembered after brief exposure. Distinctiveness should come from an ownable combination of choices, not unnecessary complexity.

Review nearby category conventions. Familiarity may help comprehension, but copying the standard symbol, palette, and type style of the category creates confusion. Reject the most crowded visual territory unless the strategy provides a strong reason to enter it.

## Simplicity and Reduction

Simplify until every remaining feature carries recognition or meaning. Do not simplify past the point of becoming generic. The goal is economical specificity.

Prefer one strong idea over several weak symbols combined. Avoid enclosing a weak symbol in a shape simply to make it feel complete.

## Symbol Construction

Start with silhouette and figure-ground behavior. Make outer contour, internal counters, and negative space read intentionally at both large and small sizes.

Use geometry as a system, not a cage. Repeated radii, angles, stroke logic, or proportions can create coherence, but optical balance outranks mathematical purity. Correct circles, diagonals, pointed forms, and asymmetric mass by eye.

Use as few anchor points as the form reasonably needs. Smooth curves should have continuous tangency, clean handles, and no flat spots. Remove accidental slivers, near-tangencies, uneven joins, and details that collapse under reduction.

## Wordmarks and Letterforms

Never stop at selecting a font. Judge the exact word as a composition.

- Adjust kerning pair by pair.
- Correct uneven color, awkward counters, repeated-letter rhythm, and terminal collisions.
- Customize only where the change reinforces recognition and remains legible.
- Preserve the underlying logic of the typeface when modifying letters.
- Test ambiguous characters, especially `I`, `l`, `1`, `O`, `0`, `rn`, and `m`.
- Avoid excessive tracking, arbitrary ligatures, and novelty substitutions that slow reading.

For a combination mark, balance symbol and wordmark by optical presence rather than bounding-box measurements. Establish a repeatable alignment and spacing relationship.

## Monograms and Lettermarks

Make every intended letter discoverable without forcing the viewer through a puzzle. Overlap, interlock, or share strokes only when the result remains clear at the smallest intended size.

Avoid defaulting to initials inside a circle or shield. If the container has no strategic or structural purpose, remove it.

## Color

Prove the mark in black and white first. Add color to reinforce personality, hierarchy, or recognition, not to compensate for weak form.

Use a restrained palette. Evaluate color in the competitive context and across screens, print, embroidery, engraving, and inexpensive one-color reproduction when relevant. Account for the fact that fluorescent, metallic, and very saturated RGB colors cannot be reproduced identically in ordinary CMYK print.

## Responsive Behavior

Do not merely shrink one lockup indefinitely. Define a responsive family when applications require it:

- Full lockup for spacious contexts
- Compact or stacked lockup for narrower contexts
- Mark-only version after recognition is established
- Simplified micro-mark for favicon or tiny UI use

Preserve the same recognition idea across all variants. Remove secondary detail before changing the core identity.

## Evaluation Rubric

Score each criterion from 1 to 5 and record the reason. Reject a concept that scores below 3 on strategic fit, distinctiveness, legibility, or scalability even if its total score is high.

| Criterion | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Strategic fit | Does the idea express the intended position and audience? |
| Distinctiveness | Can it be recognized and differentiated in its category? |
| Simplicity | Is the form economical without becoming generic? |
| Legibility | Are the name, initials, and intended symbols clear? |
| Scalability | Does it survive the smallest required use and large reproduction? |
| Versatility | Does it work in one color, reverse, print, screen, and constrained shapes? |
| Memorability | Can a viewer recall its dominant structural idea? |
| Craft | Are curves, spacing, weights, alignment, and optical corrections resolved? |

Use the scores to expose weaknesses, not to manufacture false precision. A strong strategic reason may break a convention, but it must be stated and tested.

## Common Failure Modes

- A generic category icon with no distinctive transformation
- A concept that requires a paragraph to become visible
- Fine lines or tiny gaps that disappear at practical sizes
- A wordmark that is only an unadjusted font setting
- Multiple unrelated ideas combined into one mark
- Symmetry that is mathematically exact but optically unbalanced
- Negative space that looks accidental or works only at one size
- Color or mockups concealing weak monochrome form
- A symbol that resembles a competitor more than the intended brand
- Decorative effects embedded in the core identity
- A logo family whose variants no longer look related
- Presenting trend language as strategy
