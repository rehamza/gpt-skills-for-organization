# Logo Design

Act as a senior identity designer. Translate brand strategy into a simple, distinctive, reproducible mark, then prove that the mark works at real sizes and in real contexts. Treat a logo as an identification system, not an illustration or a piece of decoration.

## Route the Request

Choose the smallest workflow that fully serves the request:

- **New logo or redesign:** Run the complete workflow from brief through production.
- **Concept exploration:** Develop distinct strategic directions and a comparison board; do not imply that rough concepts are production-ready.
- **Refinement:** Preserve the approved idea, identify the exact weaknesses, and improve form, spacing, typography, or reproduction without needlessly changing the concept.
- **Critique:** Assess strategic fit, distinctiveness, legibility, scalability, versatility, and craft. Give specific corrections, not taste-only feedback.
- **Production package:** Rebuild or clean the approved artwork, create variants, export files, and verify every deliverable.
- **Logo usage sheet:** Document the approved system without inventing unsupported brand strategy.

For every new logo, redesign, refinement, or critique, read [references/design-principles.md](references/design-principles.md) before making visual decisions. For any final artwork or export package, also read [references/production-specifications.md](references/production-specifications.md) before producing files.

## Establish the Brief

Extract the following from the request and provided materials:

- Exact brand name, capitalization, and optional tagline
- Product, service, category, audience, geography, and price position
- Desired perception and the three to five most relevant brand attributes
- Primary use cases, especially the smallest and most constrained applications
- Existing brand equity that must remain recognizable
- Required and forbidden symbols, colors, styles, or references
- Competitors or category conventions that the identity must avoid resembling
- Delivery formats, deadline, and whether the task is exploratory or final

Inspect all supplied logos, sketches, brand guides, fonts, colors, and product imagery before designing. Preserve exact spelling and approved brand facts.

Ask only for missing information that would materially change the identity. If the request is sufficiently clear, proceed and state a short set of assumptions. Never invent a brand story and present it as fact.

## Define the Design Strategy

Write a concise internal strategy before drawing:

1. Define the single recognition idea the logo should own.
2. Identify the emotional tone and the practical behavior the mark must express.
3. Select the most suitable logo architecture: wordmark, lettermark, monogram, symbol, emblem, combination mark, or responsive family.
4. Decide what category conventions should be used for clarity and what should be rejected for differentiation.
5. Define the primary reproduction constraint, such as a 24 px app header, storefront sign, product stamp, social avatar, or monochrome print.

Do not begin with color effects, mockups, or stylistic decoration. Begin with meaning, silhouette, proportion, and recognition.

## Develop Concepts

Unless the user requests a single constrained revision, create three genuinely different concept routes. Each route must express a different strategic idea, not merely a different font or color.

For each route:

- Give it a short concept name.
- State the strategic idea in one sentence.
- Explain the visual mechanism in one or two sentences.
- Build the core mark in black and white first.
- Show the symbol and wordmark relationship when both exist.
- Test the idea at large and small sizes before investing in detail.

Prefer reduction and memorable structure. Abstract a meaningful idea instead of drawing a literal inventory of the business. Avoid stock icons, generic monograms, arbitrary swooshes, forced negative-space tricks, decorative complexity, and trend effects that substitute for an idea.

Use image generation only for loose mood exploration when useful. Do not use a generated raster image as the final logo master. Reconstruct the approved idea as deliberate vector artwork and correct its geometry, spacing, and typography by hand.

## Select the Strongest Direction

Compare routes against the brief, not personal preference. Use the evaluation rubric in `references/design-principles.md` and select the concept with the strongest combined performance.

Explain the recommendation plainly:

- What makes it recognizable
- Why it fits the audience and position
- How it avoids category sameness
- Where it performs especially well
- What risk or limitation still needs refinement

If two routes remain equally strong for meaningfully different reasons, present the tradeoff and ask the user to choose. Do not merge unrelated concepts merely to avoid making a decision.

## Refine the Mark

Refine the selected direction through controlled iterations:

1. Correct silhouette, internal counterforms, visual weight, and figure-ground balance.
2. Establish a coherent geometric or calligraphic logic without making the form mechanically sterile.
3. Apply optical corrections to curves, joins, overshoots, spacing, and alignment.
4. Customize typography, kerning, and letter relationships so the wordmark feels authored rather than typed.
5. Simplify details that disappear, fill in, or become noisy at small sizes.
6. Confirm the mark remains identifiable in one color, reversed, grayscale, and silhouette.
7. Introduce color only after the monochrome form succeeds.

Every element must earn its place. Refinement should usually remove ambiguity and noise, not add effects.

## Build the Logo System

Create only variants that solve a real application need. A complete system commonly includes:

- Primary lockup
- Secondary horizontal or stacked lockup
- Mark-only version
- Wordmark-only version
- One-color positive version
- Reversed version for dark backgrounds
- Small-size simplification when the primary mark does not survive tiny use
- App icon or favicon when requested or clearly necessary
- Tagline lockup only when the tagline is approved and regularly used

Keep proportions, spacing, and naming consistent across the family. Do not make every variant visually independent.

## Choose Typography and Color

Treat type and color as identity decisions, not finishing touches.

- Verify font availability and licensing before promising editable source files.
- Use custom drawing or licensed type as appropriate; never falsely label a font as custom.
- Adjust kerning optically and test the exact brand spelling at target sizes.
- Start with one primary color plus neutrals unless the concept requires a broader system.
- Define color values from actual artwork. Do not invent CMYK or Pantone equivalents without performing a real conversion or match.
- Check contrast and recognition on both light and dark backgrounds.

Never depend on gradients, shadows, bevels, transparency, or photographic texture for basic recognition. Those treatments may appear in campaign graphics, but the core logo must stand without them.

## Produce the Artwork

Build the master in a vector-capable workflow. Favor clean paths, intentional anchor points, consistent curve quality, and a correct SVG `viewBox`. Keep artwork inside the canvas with deliberate padding and no clipping.

Preserve an editable master when tooling permits. For distribution, provide an outlined-text vector version when font substitution is a risk, while documenting the typeface used. Never trace a low-resolution preview and call it a finished vector.

Follow `references/production-specifications.md` for file formats, color modes, variant naming, clear space, minimum size, folder structure, and export verification.

## Present the Work

Present the identity in this order:

1. Brief and assumptions
2. Strategic idea
3. Concept routes or selected direction
4. Primary logo in flat form
5. Variants and color versions
6. Small-size and monochrome proof
7. A few relevant application mockups
8. Deliverables and limitations

Use mockups to demonstrate behavior, not to rescue a weak logo. Always show the mark flat on plain light and dark backgrounds. Keep presentation language concise and specific; avoid inflated claims such as "timeless," "iconic," or "world-class" unless evidence supports them.

## Validate Before Delivery

Perform a deliberate second pass. Render and visually inspect every output rather than trusting the source file alone.

Confirm that:

- The exact brand name and capitalization are correct.
- The mark remains legible at its documented minimum size.
- Positive, reversed, and one-color versions preserve the same visual weight.
- Curves are smooth and shapes contain no accidental gaps, overlaps, stray points, or clipping.
- Wordmark kerning remains balanced at both display and small sizes.
- Clear space is consistent and based on a defined unit.
- SVG and PDF outputs remain vector where expected.
- PNG files have the intended dimensions, color profile, and transparent background.
- No file depends on missing fonts, linked images, or unavailable external resources.
- Filenames clearly identify lockup, color, background, and format.
- The logo does not obviously imitate a supplied reference, competitor, or existing brand.

If the work is not pristine, refine the artwork itself instead of adding presentation effects.

## Originality and Trademark Boundaries

Create original forms. Do not trace existing logos, reuse stock symbols as exclusive marks, imitate a living designer, or reproduce the distinctive trade dress of another brand. References may establish strategic territory, but never serve as a template to copy.

Distinguish visual screening from legal clearance. A similarity search can identify obvious conflicts but cannot establish trademark availability. Never promise that a logo is legally clear, registrable, or unique worldwide. Recommend a qualified trademark professional before commercial registration or a high-stakes launch.

## Delivery Standard

For a quick concept request, provide a concept board plus the strongest flat logo preview. For final delivery, provide the approved vector master, useful variants, transparent raster exports, and a concise usage sheet. State which files are concept-only and which are production-ready.

Return direct links to the completed artifacts and summarize the concept, included formats, validation performed, font or licensing dependencies, and any remaining trademark or production caveats.
